The Cost ofGetting Big

To build muscle you need two things: protein and calories. Both cost money — and in Australia, that cost has climbed. This is the story of how to get the most gains per dollar, and what grocery inflation has done to the price of bulking.

Every chart below reads from real data: nutrient profiles from the Australian Food Composition Database joined to current Woolworths & Coles shelf prices, set against ABS food-price history. Follow the three acts — first the protein economy, then the calorie economy, then cost over time and place.

Act I

The Protein Economy

Muscle is built from protein, so the first question every lifter asks is: where is protein cheapest? The answer overturns gym-bro instinct — the best value is not on the meat counter.

Chart 01 · Bar
Protein you can buy for one dollar

Grams of protein per AUD$1, ranked across the bulking basket. Plant staples dominate; premium animal proteins sit at the bottom.

📌 Lentils deliver about 11× more protein per dollar than Atlantic salmon — yet salmon is the food most often marketed to gym-goers.
Chart 02 · Scatter (value frontier)
Price versus protein density

Each food plotted by price ($/kg) against protein per 100 g. Bubble size = protein per dollar; colour = food category. The closer to the top-left, the better the value.

📌 Whey is protein-dense but mid-priced. Eggs, milk and legumes cluster in the high-value corner — cheap and protein-rich.
Chart 03 · Lollipop
Animal vs plant: cost of 10 g protein

What it costs to buy a 10 g "protein dollar" from each food, split by source.

📌 Plant proteins are systematically cheaper per gram than animal proteins.
Chart 04 · Heatmap
Cheap protein that also carries minerals

Key micronutrients (iron, zinc, calcium) per dollar across high-value protein foods.

📌 Legumes and eggs pull double duty — cheap protein and dense in iron & zinc.
Act II

The Calorie Economy

Bulking is not only protein — it is a calorie surplus. To grow you must eat more energy than you burn, day after day. So the second question is: which foods deliver the cheapest calories?

Chart 05 · Bar
The cheapest fuel: cost per 1,000 kJ

Lower is better. This is the energy economy of bulking — the foods that fill a calorie surplus for the least money.

📌 Rice and oats are the cheapest calories money can buy. Lean meats and salmon are an expensive way to hit a surplus.
Chart 06 · Stacked bar
Where the energy comes from

Share of each food's energy from protein, fat and carbohydrate. Sorted by protein density — the most "lean-bulk friendly" foods sit at the top.

📌 The cheapest calories (rice, oats, bread) are carb-dominant; the leanest options carry their energy as protein.
Chart 07 · Quadrant scatter
The bulking sweet spot

Cost of calories (x) against cost of protein (y). The bottom-left quadrant — cheap calories and cheap protein — is where a budget bulk lives.

📌 Oats, lentils and rice anchor the sweet spot. Salmon, rump and kangaroo sit far out — flavour and variety, not value.
Chart 08 · Custom signature visual
What does a daily bulk actually cost?

Three real one-day meal plans built from the cheapest-value foods, at three budgets. Each column shows the food composition (by energy); the markers track protein and calories achieved. This is the heart of the story.

📌 You don't need to spend big to get big. A $10 day already clears 170 g protein and 3,700 kcal. Extra dollars buy more protein, not more calories — the calorie target is met cheaply, the protein target is what money raises.
Act III

The Rising Price of Gains

Knowing the cheapest foods matters more than ever, because the bill has grown. Australian food prices were flat for years — then surged. And the cost of eating big is not the same everywhere.

Chart 09 · Multi-line
Food prices over a decade (2014 = 100)

Price index for the food groups a bulker lives on. Flat through the 2010s, then a steep climb from 2021.

📌 Meat & seafood and bread & cereal rose the most — the protein and the calories both got pricier at once.
Chart 10 · Slope chart
Before the spike vs after: 2020 → 2024

A direct read of how far each food group jumped across the inflation surge. Steeper slope = sharper pain.

📌 In just four years, meat & seafood climbed from index 108 to 132 — the steepest line, and the costliest for a protein-hungry lifter.
Chart 11 · Geographic map (required)
Where bulking costs the most

Weekly cost of a high-protein bulking basket by state and territory, shaded by a relative grocery price index (Australia = 100). Albers projection.

📌 The Northern Territory pays the most — remote freight pushes the same basket roughly 18% above the national average; the southern capitals pay least.
Chart 12 · Derived comparison
The same bulk, then and now

The identical weekly bulking basket priced at 2014 versus 2025, applying the ABS food price index. Same food, same gains — higher bill.

📌 The same bulking basket now costs about 34% more than in 2014. Choosing high-value foods (Act I & II) is how lifters claw that back.

Getting big has never been about expensive food.
It is about protein and calories per dollar — and knowing where to find them.

About this visualisation

Why & Who. Built for everyday Australians who train and want to gain muscle without overspending — students, gym beginners, and budget-conscious lifters navigating rising grocery costs.

What. Three combined sources: nutrient profiles from the Australian Food Composition Database, Release 3 (FSANZ); current Woolworths & Coles shelf prices (manually compiled, 2025–26); and the food price trajectory from the ABS Consumer Price Index, Australia.

How. Vega-Lite idioms — bar, scatter, bubble, lollipop, heatmap, stacked bar, quadrant scatter, multi-line, slope chart, choropleth map, plus a custom signature "budget bulk" composite built from derived per-dollar metrics (protein/$, cost per 1,000 kJ).
