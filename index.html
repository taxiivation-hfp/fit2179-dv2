The Cost of
Getting Big

Building muscle takes two things — protein and calories — and both cost money. This is a plain-language look at where Australians get the most muscle-building food per dollar, and how much grocery prices have climbed.

A few words first, in plain English

Bulking
Eating more than you burn, on purpose, so your body has the spare energy and protein to build muscle.
Protein
The nutrient muscle is made from. Lifters aim for a set amount each day (often around 150 g).
Calories & kilojoules (kJ)
Two ways of measuring food energy. Australia uses kilojoules on labels; 1 Calorie ≈ 4.2 kJ. More energy means more fuel for growth.
"Per dollar"
Throughout, we ask how much protein or energy you actually get for each $1 spent — the value-for-money view.

Everything here comes from real data: nutrient values from the Australian Food Composition Database, joined to current Woolworths and Coles shelf prices, set against ABS food-price history. The story runs in three parts — first where protein is cheapest, then where calories are cheapest, then how the bill has changed over time and across the country.

Part one

Where protein is cheapest

Muscle is built from protein, so the first question is simple: which foods give you the most protein for your money? The answer surprises most gym-goers — the best value isn't at the meat counter.

📌 Lentils give about 11× more protein per dollar than salmon — yet salmon is the food most often sold to gym-goers as "healthy".

Price versus how protein-rich a food is

Each dot is a food, placed by its price (across) and how much protein it packs per 100 g (up). The bigger the dot, the better the value. The best buys sit toward the top-left: protein-rich but cheap.

📌 Eggs, milk and legumes sit in the cheap-and-protein-rich corner. Whey protein is very protein-rich but mid-priced; salmon and steak are costly per gram.

📌 Notice the top rows: lentils, chickpeas and eggs deliver cheap protein and are rich in iron and zinc.
Part two

Where calories are cheapest

Bulking isn't only about protein — you also need surplus energy, day after day, for your body to grow. So the second question is: which foods deliver the cheapest calories?

Chart 5 · Bar chart
The cheapest fuel for a bulk

Cost of 1,000 kJ of energy (about 240 Calories) from each food. Shorter bars are cheaper energy.

📌 Rice and oats are the cheapest calories you can buy. Lean meats and salmon are an expensive way to fill an energy surplus.
Chart 6 · Stacked bar chart
What kind of energy each food gives

For twelve key bulking foods, the share of energy coming from protein, fat and carbohydrate. Sorted so the most protein-heavy foods sit at the top.

📌 The cheapest calories (rice, oats, bread) are mostly carbohydrate; the leanest foods carry their energy as protein. A good bulk uses both.
Chart 7 · Scatter plot (four quadrants)
The value sweet spot

Cheap calories run left; cheap protein run down. The bottom-left box is the bargain zone — foods that are cheap for both.

📌 Oats, rice and lentils anchor the sweet spot. Salmon, rump steak and kangaroo sit far out — they're for flavour and variety, not value.

Part three

The rising price of gains

Choosing good-value food matters more than ever, because the bill has grown. Australian food prices were flat for years, then climbed sharply — and the cost of groceries differs a little around the country.

Chart 9 · Multi-line chart
A decade of food prices

Price level for the food groups a lifter lives on, with 2014 set to 100. The whole decade in one view: flat, then a steep climb from 2021.

📌 Prices barely moved for years — then everything rose together from 2021. The next chart zooms in on that jump.
Chart 10 · Slope chart
Who climbed fastest during the spike

Just the two protein-and-calorie groups a bulker cares about most, compared from 2020 to 2024. The steeper the line, the harder that food hit the budget.

📌 Bread & cereal and meat & seafood both jumped over 20% in four years — the carbs and the protein got pricier at the same time.
Chart 11 · Map (required)
Does bulking cost more in some cities?

Each state is shaded by its capital city's grocery price index (national average = 100); the dark circle on each capital is sized by the weekly cost of the same high-protein basket. Drawn on an Albers map projection. Hover any state or city for details.

📌 The honest answer: not by much. Groceries vary only about 10% across the country — Melbourne, Canberra and Sydney are dearest; Darwin and Hobart cheapest. Supermarket pricing is remarkably uniform.
Chart 12 · Before-and-after comparison
The same weekly shop, 2014 versus 2025

The identical bulking basket, priced eleven years apart using the ABS food index. Same food, same gains — bigger bill.

📌 The same bulking week now costs about 34% more than in 2014. Choosing the high-value foods from Parts one and two is how lifters claw that back.

Getting big has never really been about expensive food.
It's about protein and calories per dollar — and knowing where to find them.

Why & who

Built for everyday Australians who train and want to gain muscle without overspending — students, gym beginners and budget-conscious lifters facing rising grocery costs.

What (data)

Three sources, combined: nutrient profiles from the Australian Food Composition Database, Release 3 (FSANZ); current Woolworths & Coles shelf prices (compiled 2025–26); food-price history from the ABS Consumer Price Index, Australia; and, for the map, the Numbeo grocery price index for capital cities (used because the ABS publishes food prices only as an eight-city average, not per state).

How (design)

Ten Vega-Lite idioms — bar, scatter, lollipop, heatmap, stacked bar, four-quadrant scatter, multi-line, slope chart and choropleth map — plus a custom, interactive "daily bulk" builder. Value metrics (protein per dollar, cost per 1,000 kJ) are derived from the joined data.
